Setting Your Diamond Budget

There's no rule for how much a diamond "should" cost — despite what you may have heard. Here's how to set a number you're comfortable with, then spend it where it actually shows.

~6 min read · Updated July 2026

Ignore the "months of salary" rule

You've probably heard that you should spend one, two, or three months' salary on a ring. It's worth knowing where that came from: it's a marketing slogan, popularized by a diamond advertising campaign in the 20th century, not financial advice. It exists to anchor you to a bigger number.

The right budget is simply what you can comfortably spend without going into debt or straining your finances. A diamond is a purchase, not an investment (more on that below), so treat the budget like any other big discretionary purchase: pick a number that fits your life, and let that drive the diamond — not the other way around.

A healthier framing: decide what you're comfortable spending first, then use the 4Cs to get the best-looking stone at that number. You never have to hit a "rule," and no one can tell what you paid by looking at the ring.

Where the money goes

A diamond's price is driven by the 4Cs — carat, cut, color, and clarity — plus shape and whether it's natural or lab-grown. The single most important thing to understand about budgeting is that these four don't reward your money equally. Some visibly improve the stone; others you're literally paying for a certificate you can't see with your eyes. Spend where it shows.

Spend here: Cut

Cut is the one C you should never cheap out on. It's what makes a diamond sparkle, and a poorly cut stone looks dull no matter how good its other grades are. Prioritize an Excellent or Ideal cut — it does more for the diamond's beauty than any other factor.

Save here: Clarity

Clarity measures tiny internal flaws, most of which are invisible without magnification. You almost never need Flawless or even VVS. In most stones, a VS2 or a well-chosen SI1 is "eye-clean" — meaning no flaws are visible to the naked eye — at a fraction of the price. Buy the lowest clarity that's still eye-clean and put the savings into cut or carat.

Save here (mostly): Color

Color runs from D (colorless) down through the alphabet toward faint yellow. The difference between a D and a G is nearly impossible to see once the stone is set, but the price gap is large. G–H is the sweet spot for most people — it looks white to the eye and costs much less than the top grades. (Exception: emerald and Asscher cuts show color more, so nudge up a grade there.)

Spend to taste: Carat

Carat is weight, and it's what most people mean by "size." It's also where prices jump hardest — and they jump at round numbers. A stone just under a milestone weight looks almost identical to one just over it, but costs noticeably less.

The under-the-milestone trick: a 0.90 ct looks virtually identical to a 1.00 ct but can cost 10–20% less, because demand (and therefore price) spikes right at 1.00, 1.50, and 2.00 carats. Buying at 0.90, 1.4, or 1.9 ct is one of the easiest ways to save real money with no visible difference.

Three levers that stretch the budget furthest

LeverWhat it does
Lab-grown over naturalThe biggest one. A lab-grown diamond is a real diamond that typically costs a large fraction less for the same specs — often letting you jump a full size or quality tier. See our lab vs. natural guide.
A fancy shape over roundRound is the most expensive shape per carat. Oval, cushion, pear, and radiant cost less for the same weight and often look bigger. See the shape guide.
Buying just under a carat milestone0.90 instead of 1.00, 1.9 instead of 2.0 — near-identical look, meaningfully lower price.

Stack two or three of these and the same budget can buy a dramatically better-looking stone. A lab-grown, 1.9 ct oval with an excellent cut and G color will out-dazzle a much smaller, "perfect-on-paper" natural round at the same price.

Don't forget the setting

The diamond is usually the biggest line item, but the ring setting is a separate cost — anywhere from a couple hundred dollars for a simple band to several thousand for an elaborate design in platinum. Decide roughly how you want to split your total between stone and setting before you fall in love with either, so the two together land within budget.

A quick word on "resale value"

Be clear-eyed: diamonds are generally poor financial investments. Retail markups mean most diamonds resell for well below what you paid, and lab-grown stones in particular have little resale value as prices keep falling. This isn't a reason not to buy one — just a reason to buy it for the ring and the moment, and to size your budget accordingly rather than thinking of it as money you'll get back.

Putting it together

  • Pick a comfortable total first — ignore the salary "rules."
  • Protect the cut — Excellent/Ideal is where beauty comes from.
  • Save on clarity and color — buy eye-clean (around VS2/SI1) and G–H, not the top of the scale.
  • Use the big levers — consider lab-grown, a fancy shape, and a weight just under a milestone.
  • Budget the setting separately so the total fits.
  • Then compare — the same specs vary a lot in price across retailers, and that spread is free money.
