How to Read a Diamond Certificate

A grading report is an independent lab's verdict on exactly what you're buying. Once you know how to read one, you can compare stones honestly — and spot the ones that are quietly overpriced.

~6 min read · Updated July 2026

A diamond "certificate" — more accurately a grading report — is issued by an independent gemological laboratory that has examined the specific stone. It's the closest thing to an objective, comparable description of a diamond, which is why you should never buy a significant diamond without one.

Which labs to trust

Not all labs grade to the same standard. The most respected are:

LabBest known for
GIA (Gemological Institute of America)The industry benchmark, especially for natural diamonds. Widely regarded as the strictest and most consistent.
IGI (International Gemological Institute)The dominant lab for lab-grown diamonds; also grades natural stones. Fast and widely used.
AGS / GCALSmaller but well-regarded labs, known for rigorous cut grading.

An important nuance: labs can grade with slightly different strictness. It's generally accepted that GIA grades most conservatively, so a "G / VS1" from one lab isn't always identical to a "G / VS1" from another. When comparing two natural diamonds, comparing GIA-to-GIA is the most apples-to-apples. Be cautious with in-house or store-brand "certificates," which aren't independent.

Reading the report, line by line

Most reports share the same core fields. Here's what each one tells you:

Report number

A unique ID for that exact stone. On GIA reports it's often laser-inscribed on the diamond's girdle. You can look this number up on the lab's website to confirm the report is genuine (more on that below).

Shape and cutting style

e.g., "Round Brilliant," "Emerald Cut." This describes the outline and facet arrangement, not the quality of the cut.

Measurements

The stone's dimensions in millimeters (e.g., 6.40 × 6.44 × 3.96 mm). Useful for judging how large it will look face-up and, for fancy shapes, its length-to-width ratio.

Carat weight

Exact weight to the hundredth of a carat. Watch for stones sitting just under a "magic weight" (like 0.98 ct) — they can look identical to a 1.00 ct for less money.

The 4Cs: color, clarity, and (for rounds) cut grade

The heart of the report. Color (D–Z), clarity (FL–I3), and — for round brilliants — an overall cut grade (Excellent to Poor). Fancy shapes usually won't show an overall cut grade, so you rely on the proportions and a video. If you're fuzzy on these, see our 4Cs guide.

Polish and symmetry

Two "finish" grades (Excellent to Poor). They describe how cleanly the facets are polished and how precisely they line up. Very Good or better is ideal; these have a modest effect on sparkle and price.

Fluorescence

How much the diamond glows under UV light (None, Faint, Medium, Strong, Very Strong). In most stones it's harmless and invisible in daily life; occasionally strong fluorescence can make a very white diamond look slightly hazy — but it can also make a faintly-colored stone appear whiter. Strong fluorescence often comes at a discount, which can be a value opportunity if the stone still looks crisp.

Proportions diagram & clarity plot

Many reports include a side-profile diagram (table %, depth %, crown and pavilion angles) that drives how well the stone reflects light, and a "clarity plot" mapping the location of inclusions. The plot is gold: an SI1 with an inclusion tucked near the edge can be a far better buy than one with a mark under the table.

Verify the report is real

Anyone can print a PDF. Confirm the report exists and matches:

  • Go to the issuing lab's official report-check tool (GIA and IGI both have one on their websites).
  • Enter the report number and confirm the grades on screen match the document you were given.
  • For higher-value stones, ask whether the report number is laser-inscribed on the girdle so it can be matched to the physical stone.

Red flags: no report at all; an in-house or "appraisal-only" certificate presented as a lab report; a report number that doesn't verify on the lab's site; or grades that look too good for a suspiciously low price. When in doubt, prefer GIA (natural) or IGI (lab-grown) and verify the number yourself.

What actually matters most on the report

If you only remember three things: prioritize the cut grade and proportions (they drive sparkle), aim for the lowest eye-clean clarity using the plot and a video, and verify the report number before you pay. Everything else is fine-tuning.
