Eye-Clean Diamonds: The Clarity Trick That Saves You Money

Learn one word before you buy and you can save hundreds — sometimes thousands — on clarity you'd never see anyway. That word is "eye-clean."

~4 min read · Updated November 2026

Clarity is the 4C where buyers most often overspend. The grading scale runs from Flawless (FL) down through VVS, VS, SI, and I — and each step up costs more. But here's the catch: clarity is graded under 10× magnification, and you're never going to wear your ring under a microscope. What actually matters is whether the diamond looks clean to your eye.

What "eye-clean" means

An eye-clean diamond is one with no inclusions visible to the naked eye at normal viewing distance (about 8–12 inches). It may have tiny inclusions a grader found under magnification, but you simply can't see them in real life. To the eye, an eye-clean SI1 and a Flawless diamond look identical — yet the price gap between them can be enormous.

The money move: don't buy the highest clarity grade — buy the lowest grade that's still eye-clean for your specific stone. That's where the value lives.

For most shoppers, VS2 or SI1 is the smart target. These grades are eye-clean the large majority of the time while costing far less than VVS or Flawless. (New to the grades? Our 4Cs guide explains the full scale.)

Always check the actual stone

Clarity is the one C you should verify with your own eyes, because inclusions vary stone to stone. Two SI1 diamonds can look different — one flawless to the eye, one with a faintly visible spot. Before buying, look at a high-resolution photo or video of the exact diamond (not a stock image), and check the clarity plot on its grading report to see where inclusions sit. An inclusion near the edge is far less noticeable than one dead-center under the table.

Does shape affect eye-cleanliness?

Yes, a little. Brilliant-cut shapes like round, oval, and cushion hide inclusions well because their sparkle masks small flaws — so you can often go lower on clarity. Step cuts like emerald and Asscher have large open facets that reveal inclusions more easily, so they reward a slightly higher clarity grade. See our shape guide for how each cut behaves.
